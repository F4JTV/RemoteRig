1. How it works

Two programs. The server runs next to the transceiver: it shares its audio both ways, keys its PTT, and drives it over CAT when that is available. The client runs wherever the operator is — on a computer or on a phone — and gives the rig back to them.

Two network channels connect them. Control goes over TCP, port 7300 by default: authentication, rig state, commands. Audio and PTT go over UDP, port 7301: a lost datagram is forgotten rather than resent, which is what speech wants. The PTT travels on both at once, so a loss never leaves the rig transmitting.

Latency. On a local network, expect around 65 ms end to end with Opus at a 10 ms frame, a little less with PCM. The jitter buffer, which is adjustable, dominates that budget.

What the station needs

  • An audio interface between rig and computer: a USB sound card, a digital interface, or the transceiver's built-in audio.
  • For CAT, a control cable and the matching Hamlib model.
  • Without CAT, a plain serial port whose RTS or DTR keys the PTT. You lose frequency and mode, not speech.

2. Installation

Linux — Ubuntu, Debian, Raspberry Pi OS

sudo apt install ./remoterig_1.0.0_amd64.deb

Use apt, not dpkg -i: the dependencies come along. The package installs the server, both clients, their desktop entries, icons and man pages.

One package is one architecture: amd64 for a PC, arm64 for a 64-bit Raspberry Pi. To build your own:

./make_deb.sh
To drive a rig over CAT, your account must belong to the dialout group: 
sudo usermod -a -G dialout $USER
Log out and back in for it to take effect.

Windows

Run the installer and follow the steps. It offers to open the firewall ports: accept, or no client will reach the station.

Android

Install the signed APK. On a Samsung phone, Auto Blocker prevents installation from outside the store: turn it off for the installation, under Settings → Security and privacy.

Audio tab

Pick the input — what the rig receives — and the output — what goes out on transmit. Rescan audio devices picks up a card plugged in after launch.

The PTT tone suits interfaces that key the radio when they detect audio on the right channel — Digirig among them. The output is then opened in stereo: the modulation stays on the left, the tone goes out on the right, and it starts before the first useful sample. The frequency is adjustable, 2200 Hz by default. Check that the radio is fed from the left channel only, otherwise the tone would be transmitted.

Frame size trades latency against robustness. 480 samples, that is 10 ms, suits most cases; go down to 240 on a fast local network, up to 960 if the audio breaks up.

Network tab

The TCP and UDP ports, the password, and two guards:

  • Reject clients that do not encrypt — turn this on as soon as the station is reachable from outside.
  • Refuse transmission out of band — the server compares the emitted spectrum against the transmit ranges the rig declares, and blocks the PTT outside them. Turn it off for a transverter, whose working range is not the rig's.

Without a desktop

A remote station does not need a desktop. Configure once with a display, then:

remoterig-server --headless
remoterig-server --headless -v          # and log frequency changes
remoterig-server --config rig2.ini --headless

The package installs a systemd user unit:

systemctl --user enable --now remoterig-server
journalctl --user -u remoterig-server -f
sudo loginctl enable-linger $USER       # run without an open session

On startup the server prints the addresses the remote operator should type, and warns when the password is empty.

4. The client

Connecting

Enter the station's address, port and password, then Connect. Leave the UDP field on auto: the server announces its own audio port. Force it only behind a NAT rule that remaps the number.

Auto-reconnect rebuilds the link on its own after a drop: first attempt after one second, then the delay doubles up to thirty. During the wait the button becomes Cancel.

Audio

SettingWhat it does
CodecOpus low latency for speech, 16-bit PCM for data modes, where any compression hurts. Switching does not drop the link.
JitterReceive buffer. Raise it if the audio breaks up, lower it to gain latency.
Volume, mic levelAdjustable live, even mid-transmission.

Microphone shaping

Four presets match the shaping to the microphone in use. The chain cuts below 300 Hz, lifts presence around 2 kHz, cuts above 3.2 kHz and compresses at 3:1. The transmit meter shows the gain reduction applied.

Set the mic level so the peak mark reaches the amber zone without lighting the clip indicator to the right of the meter. The meter's three zones — green to 55 %, amber to 80 %, red beyond — are fixed: their place on the scale means something absolute, and the peak holds for a second and a half before falling back.

Raw CAT command

A field sends a sequence to the rig untouched, for the settings no driver covers. The terminator is added when missing, nothing else is interpreted, and the answer comes back in the log. It has its own CAT tab in the desktop client, with a short guide to the command syntax and its own log; on the phone it sits in the drawer.

Every frequency, mode and VFO change is read back after being written. Rigs round to their VFO step or refuse silently; when what they report differs from what was asked, the log says both.

5. Operating

Transmitting

Hold the transmit button, or the space bar on the desktop client. Releasing drops the PTT after a short tail, set on the server, which keeps the ends of sentences from being clipped.

The button greys out when transmission is impossible: no link, tuning cycle running, keyer sending, or frequency out of band.

Frequency, bands, VFO

The band buttons are built from the transmit ranges the rig declares, so each transceiver shows its own. Clicking the frequency display opens an entry that accepts 14.074, 14074 or 14074000.

Modes and filter width

The mode list comes from the rig, so each shows its own. Beside it, the three filter widths Hamlib normalises for the current mode — wide, normal, narrow — in kilohertz. Picking one applies the width without changing mode; the rig answers with what it actually kept, and the list follows it.

Antenna tuner

The Tune button appears only when the rig accepts the command and is answering on CAT. During the cycle the indicator reads TUNE and the PTT is locked: the rig is already transmitting.

SWR

The gauge below the S-meter appears only when the rig reports SWR. It is only measurable while transmitting; the last reading is held after unkeying, long enough to read, and cleared on the next transmission. Past 2:1 the figure turns red.

Morse

Two routes, depending on what the rig accepts.

The rig keys itself. The text goes out over CAT and the rig's own electronic keyer generates the elements. The simplest route when it works.

It does not work everywhere. On Yaesu HF sets, the keyer command only replays the rig's internal memories: sending AGN? plays memory 1 whatever the text. Five buttons therefore call those memories directly, numbered 1 to 5. They are recorded on the rig itself — menus 04-07 to 04-11 on an FT-891 — and hold up to 50 characters each.

The server generates the elements. Tick Generate CW here and key a serial line on the Rig tab, choose the port and the line, DTR or RTS, wired to the KEY jack. The network never takes part in the spacing: only the text travels, and the rhythm is produced next to the radio.

The keying port must be free: a serial port cannot be opened twice, so it cannot be the one the rig control already holds. Transceivers that expose two USB serial ports, and interfaces such as the SCU-17, leave you one for this.

Correction shortens every keyed element, never the silences, to make up for the time the rig takes to raise its carrier. Start at zero and raise it only if your correspondents report clipped dots. The line can be inverted, and the PTT held for the whole message on rigs that do not key themselves.

The panel opens from the dot-and-dash button in the top bar: speed, six software memories, the rig's own five, a free-text field, and a stop kept within reach. Software memories use %c for your callsign, entered once in the settings.

Band-edge guard

The server judges the emitted spectrum, not the carrier. In USB the emission sits above the displayed frequency, in LSB below. At exactly 7.200 000 MHz, LSB stays inside the 40 m band while USB runs past the edge. When transmission is blocked, the client shows the computed limits.

6. Data modes

The client publishes a rigctld interface on 127.0.0.1:4532. WSJT-X, fldigi or JS8Call then drive the remote rig as if it were local.

  1. Switch the codec to 16-bit PCM: Opus is made for voice and damages digital modulations.
  2. Enable the rigctld interface — a checkbox on the desktop, a switch in the drawer on the phone.
  3. In the data-mode software, select the Hamlib NET rigctl rig and the address 127.0.0.1:4532.
  4. Route the audio between the software and the client: a virtual cable on Windows, a null-sink module under PipeWire or PulseAudio.
Turn microphone shaping off for data. The compressor and presence filter are made for speech and would degrade an FT8 signal.

7. Security and networking

The password is never sent: authentication is challenge-response, with PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 over 60,000 rounds. Optional encryption then protects control and audio with ChaCha20, with an integrity check.

A session token derived from the password accompanies every PTT command: a third party watching the UDP traffic cannot key the station by replaying a datagram.

Exposing a station on the internet calls for care. Prefer a tunnel — VPN or WireGuard — to port forwarding. If you forward anyway, require encryption and choose a long password.

Client watchdog

A client that vanishes without closing its connection — a phone switched off, a Wi-Fi link that drops — would otherwise leave the rig transmitting: TCP does not notice for tens of minutes. The server therefore times everything it hears. Two seconds of silence with the PTT down stops the transmission; fifteen seconds releases the station for another client.

8. Troubleshooting

SymptomCause and cure
The microphone is missing from the list On Linux, PortAudio skips cards already held by PipeWire. Declare a named PCM in ~/.asoundrc pointing at hw:x,0. Use remoterig-server --list-audio to find the numbers.
"no CAT" although the rig is on Wrong serial port, baud rate or Hamlib model. Check membership of the dialout group too.
The tune button or the keyer does not appear Both require CAT that answers, not merely a rig that was opened. The log lists the detected capabilities right after connecting.
"Invalid configuration" when opening the rig If the log shows cannot get host "ttyUSB3", Hamlib took the port name for a network address. Fixed since 1.0.0: the server now passes /dev/ttyUSB3. On an older build, type the full path.
CW sends something other than the text The rig is replaying one of its internal memories instead of keying the text. Use the five memory buttons, or turn on the server's own keyer.
CM108 PTT does not work on Linux Permissions on /dev/hidraw*. Install the udev rule shipped with the project, then unplug and replug the interface.
CM108 PTT does not work on Windows Hamlib implements it for Linux only: it opens /dev/hidraw, which has no Windows equivalent. Use the PTT tone instead.
The audio breaks up Raise the jitter buffer, then the frame size. On a busy network, switch to Opus if you were on PCM.
Cannot transmit, "out of band" The emitted spectrum falls outside the ranges the rig declares. The message gives the computed limits. For a transverter, turn the guard off on the server.
ALSA messages scroll past at startup PortAudio probes every plugin the system declares, including ones that match nothing. Harmless.
